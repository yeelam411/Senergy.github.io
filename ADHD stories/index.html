Introduction

ADHD (Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder) is a neurodevelopmental disorder that affects a person's ability to regulate their attention, emotions, and impulses. It is not a choice, and it is not the result of "bad parenting." It is caused by differences in how the brain's chemical messengers, like dopamine help us focus and stay organized.

The Three Faces of ADHD:

  • Inattentive: Often called "daydreamers." They may struggle to follow long instructions, lose their belongings frequently, or get easily distracted by unrelated thoughts.
  • Hyperactive-Impulsive: These students feel an internal "motor" that won't stop. They may fidget, talk excessively, or act before thinking about the consequences.
  • Combined: This is the most common type, where the student shows a mix of both inattention and hyperactivity.

Key Characteristics of ADHD:

  • Working Memory: Holding information in mind while doing a task.
  • Emotional Control: Managing frustrations when things get difficult.
  • Task Initiation: Starting a project without procrastinating.
  • Time Management: Understanding how long a task will actually take.

How Parents Can Help:

  • Externalize Time: Use physical timers, clocks, or alarms. Students with ADHD often have "time blindness" and need visual cues to see time passing.
  • The "Power of Now": Give instructions one at a time. Long lists of chores or homework tasks can feel like a "wall of noise" to an ADHD brain.
  • Positive Reinforcement: ADHD students receive significantly more "corrections" than their peers. Focus on catching them doing something right to build their self-esteem.
  • Movement is Fuel: Allow for "heavy work" or movement breaks. For many with ADHD, moving their body actually helps their brain concentrate.

A Note to Students!!!

Your brain is built for speed. Many people with ADHD are "out-of-the-box" thinkers, high-energy creators, and excellent crisis managers. While sitting still in a classroom might feel like a struggle right now, your ability to hyper-focus on things you love is a major strength. You aren't "broken"—you just have a "Ferrari engine" for a brain with "bicycle brakes." Learning to use those brakes takes practice, but your engine will take you amazing places!
