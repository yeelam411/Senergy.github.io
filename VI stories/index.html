Introduction

Visual Impairment is a term used to describe a loss of sight that cannot be fully corrected by ordinary glasses or contact lenses.

Vision loss is not a reflection of intelligence. Students with VI process information differently, often through high-contrast visuals, touch (Braille), or audio, but they have the same capacity to learn and succeed as their sighted peers.

Types of Visual Impairment:

  • Low Vision: Permanent vision loss that interferes with daily tasks. It may involve tunnel vision, blurred vision, or blind spots.
  • Legal Blindness: A level of vision loss significant enough to require specialized support and modifications.
  • Total Blindness: A complete lack of light perception and form.
  • Color Blindness: Difficulty distinguishing between certain colors (usually red and green), which can impact reading coded maps or charts.

Tools for Learning:

  • Magnification: Using digital magnifiers or large-print books.
  • Braille: A system of raised dots that allows students to read and write by touch.
  • Screen Readers: Software that reads out loud the text displayed on a computer screen.
  • Tactile Graphics: Raised-line drawings that allow students to feel maps or geometric shapes.

How Parents Can Help:

  • Narrate the world: Describe things clearly. Instead of saying put it over there, say put it on the blue table to your left.
  • Organize for consistency: Keep furniture and belongings in the same place. Unexpected obstacles can be a safety hazard for a child with VI.
  • High contrast is key: Use black markers on white paper, or light-colored plates on dark placemats to help your child see edges and boundaries.
  • Encourage sensory exploration: Involve other senses. Let your child smell ingredients while cooking or feel textures of different leaves in the park.

A Note to Students!!!

You see the world in your own unique way. While others rely mostly on their eyes, you are learning to use your ears, your hands, and your memory in incredibly sharp ways. Having a visual impairment might mean you navigate life with a cane, a guide dog, or a screen reader, but those are just different ways of getting to the same destination. Many brilliant people, from musicians like Stevie Wonder to activists like Helen Keller, have shown that vision is about much more than what your eyes see.
